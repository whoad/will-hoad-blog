To maximise the value of what we do in a finite time constrained world, we must make hard decisions on what options out of many we think are going to give us the most valuable outcomes. One long standing popular approach to deciding what options to pick over others is MoSCoW, but it has some often-missed problems that make it notably worse than sticking to a prioritised list.

### MoSCoW

MoSCoW is a prioritisation approach that divides up available options into categories of Must, Should, Could, and Wont.

The challenge with MoSCoW is how its simplicity and language make it great to go from chaos to something, but it quickly becomes more of a hinderance than aid to making hard decisions and accepting reality. 

#### MoSCoW problems:

1. Categorisation only does half a job of informing on what the most valuable work is. It avoids the hard decisions of how different options within a criteria compare that are really needed to focus on the most valuable work. 
   **Seen as:** Team members trying to deliver all the Musts at once due to their equal criticality and failing to finish. Bloat of Must work. Harder to deliver value early.
   
2. The language of the word “Must” is more likely to be based on what individuals see it to mean, rather that of the group that did the prioritisation. This makes working with stakeholders’ harder when MoSCoW is used in any non-trivial setting. Customers version of Must is going to be what they want, vs what a team may think it essential to keep the lights on.
   **Seen as:** Team calls Customer A’s Musts Shoulds as they have decided customer B’s work is more important this quarter. Being told their work is only a “should” upsets the customer triggers debate with seniors pressuring the team about what counts as a Must and to re categorise (rather than coming together create a shared understanding). 
   
3. MoSCoWs simplicity too easily leads to becoming a flawed time bound project commitment. The priority assessment is only true for the time in which it was conducted, if it is not changeable repeatable within a time box it acts as a lock on Cost, Time & Scope. This lock on the Iron Triangle is the critical dysfunction of traditional non iterative project management approaches.
   **Seen as:** Committing teams to deliver all the Musts and Should by the end of the quarter but not repeating the MoSCoW assessment when new options arise, leading to either over commitment or hidden work.
   
4. MoSCoWs category definitions are weak, making it too easy to conflate value categorisation with time bounded definitions. It can’t be used to independently define all the most valuable work, and then separately be used for time bound deliveries. These are 2 separate uses but are often missed. This encourages conversations towards if an option fits in a category or not, rather than the nuance of the value it can bring. 
   **Seen as:** Using MoSCoW on a product to decide what features are Musts and Shoulds. After doing this then using the same assessment for what Must & Should be delivered in the next time box. 

Fundamentally, all the above points lead to making the hard decisions of value prioritisation harder.

While some of the points above could be argued as failure of implementation, the fact that it is so easy to not get right without realising the downsides makes it a risky approach.

### Single Prioritised list

There are many ways to analyse, research and in the end decide on what options are more valuable than others. The crux is ensuring the result is a specific and clear cut as possible. The representation that does this best is a single ordered list of options starting from the highest priority and working its way down to the lowest. 
#### Benefits over MoSCoW

1. linear lists enforce a relative comparison as all options are prioritised against each other, rather than on if each one individually meets an external criteria. This relative prioritisation dramatically better at building a shared understanding about the options as it encourages many different elements to be considered, not just that of a MoSCoW criteria.
   **Seen as:** The team actively exploring the customer benefit of a new feature against support or tech debt. Exploration leads to better understanding and articulation of technical debt turning into a customer desired feature.
   
2. Use of a visual constraint having one option physically above another is far harder to misconstrue. better avoids debate around categorisation and better forces the hard conversation on what goes above or below what. 
   **Seen as:** Customer A having to actively debate why their option should be done sooner than Customer B’s option above it. (rather than if their option fits a category or not)
   
3. Stakeholder inquisition of a single list is more likely to drive efficient breakdown of work.
   **Seen as:** Customer A seeing Customer B’s options are both higher up the list and have larger scope than their own. Customer A influences a breakdown of all the work so both A & B get smaller more critical options done quicker.
   
4. A prioritised list is not intrinsically time bound, and being a relative measure makes it easier to see if its order is still true or not. It lends itself better than MoSCoW to being continuously prioritised over time. 
   **Seen as:** Customer look to see which of their options have been delivered part way through a delivery, and notice that the next one is no longer as useful as the following one so requests a re-order. With MoSCoW the customer would have seen the categories and been less likely to downgrade an option for fear it may not happen

Be aware that much like MoSCoW, being such a simple tool makes additions to the approach easy. Splitting the list in some way be it by product, customer, or even MoSCoW could muddy the water by hiding options or having competing priority lists. The difference here is that the ordered list is still one of the most powerful outputs of a priority discussion regardless of approach, while MoSCoW only gets you part way.
