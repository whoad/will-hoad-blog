## Overview

This month Nigel Baker came to Cheltenham to present an [Agile Glos](https://www.eventbrite.co.uk/o/120961278045) on the past, present, and future of Agile. As always with any talk write up, this post is a mix of Nigel's points and my own thoughts and reflections mixed in.

His central premise for me was around the waxing and waning of big one-size-fits-all methodologies to solve all your delivery problems. Agile was born from a grassroots rejection of heavyweight methodologies that failed to deliver. It made massive positive change to teams’ ability to adapt and succeed within their context, as well as triggered a gold rush of methods and certifications. 20 years on though modern working has become worse than it ever was with employee surveillance, reduced autonomy, and that gold rush has taken us back to the same bad heavy methodologies that failed us before but rebadged as SaFE & Jira.

"Agile" is dead & toxic.

This is just a word though, what isn't dead are the people, the values, and the realities proven time and time again to better deliver.
* Work in reality and the real context, not the fabled best practice
* Collaborate and influence through informal connection
* Cut unnecessary and low value proces
* Focus on value delivered rather than management metric
* Keep learning through experimentation
We succeed in this modern working landscape through informal networks, experimentation and starting the odd coup to make the change needed.

“agile” values are alive and well, keep adapting to your context.

Aside from the content, I was in awe of his deeply human, genuine, and hilarious presentation ability. It felt like a true masterclass demonstration which if I can take on even a drop of I would be absolutely winning.

This is exactly why we have CACI Agile Guild and are still building agile services, because we will only succeed by working with each other in our own context, so the more we collaborate the better it will be!

## Depth

Control of our environment and reality has been at the basics of business practices and life in general since the beginning. Your best chance of survival is to make sure there's food in your belly and you can get more food tomorrow. If you have this certainty, you feel happy. If you don't you get anxious and try to take control. Humans not only crave certainty but can have physical negative reactions to a lack of it. There is an experiment where people were offered either an electric shock now or the possibility of one later and more people wanted it now rather than having it hanging over their heads.

### Past

Go back to the 80s and 90s and you'll find the structured method movement, which was born out of trying to create more certainty within a business environment where everyone was "winging it". The problem being that in trying to create more certainty, they produced more and more methods to cover each situation and the approach got thicker and thicker, the end result's been the Rational Unified Process. This was heavily driven by a company called Rational, who were selling a management tool to let you deliver it.

This environment is what triggered the formation of the Agile Manifesto. A range of experts from a range of different fields (from Scrum, from Extreme Programming, and others) came together to work out how to fix this focus on RUP, which wasn't addressing the real problems of delivering software.  
Each of these experts came with a different thing they wanted to fix. Scrum hated big upfront planning. Extreme programming hated big upfront design. Nigel focused however on Alistair Cockburn, whos point of being part of the manifesto was his opposition to large prescriptive "Methodology Armories" big peddled by industry as the paid for solution to all your problems. He saw the damage and failures coming from large contextless approaches, and wanted to drive people, collaboration and adaptive thinking. _foreshadowing_

### Today

While for me I feel there's a lot of nuance to where we are today and I can't get it down in the next few paragraphs, Nigel put it quite directly: Agile is dead.

Nigel was a part of the initial growth of Agile as an approach alongside these other signatories, and of building up ideas and practices from the bottom up as hobbyists meeting in pubs focused on supporting each other through informal networks. What started as a collection of people sharing their experiences and expertise to build something better grew into a monolithic gold rush of selling courses, certificates, building new methodologies, A whole industry blooming out of getting certifications so they could sell more certifications, till in the end, the creation of SaFE and Jira.

Anyone with any understanding of Agile and its values and purpose has been calling out SaFE from its inception as fundamentally unaligned heavyweight methodology working against the principles. People who have to use it, regardless of their background recognise that it has lots of problems and is incredibly bureaucratic. The problem is that they've been able to sell and market this monolith as an "Agile" product to C-suite and traditionally minded leaders as a way of creating certainty.  
Jira has followed a similar path from starting out as a team-oriented issue tracking tool that was received incredibly well, to a behemoth that is now "the" way that you need to manage your work. It's the classic case of the tool leading ways of working rather than the people and their needs.  
These together have made "Agile" toxic.

An even stronger driver of this though is the modern working and business environment of Tech Bros 'hero' leaders decreasing autonomy and increasing micromanagement. Nigel feels work is the most hostile its been in his lifetime. At least back before agile he wasn't been tracked through mouse wiggles, threats of mass layoffs as a share value increasing technique, or nano task time maximisation like the ones that forced amazon warehouse pickers to pee in bottles as they didn't have time to get to the loo. This leans a lot into todays political picture as well, but that's a whole other beast.

Nigel's point on Agile being dead, is not the values themselves but because we are back to where we were before but worse. We have large prescriptive methodologies just like the rational unified process, with enforced tools (Rational / Jira) leading our approaches. Nigel even points out that one of the SaFE training certificates is actually just a Rational Unified Process one rebranded.

This is exactly what Alistair Cockburn and others set out to get away from when writing the Agile Manifesto.

### Future

So, where do we go next? Back to the pub! We need to go back to our informal networks, share ideas, experiments and collaborate to the next ideas that can improve our commercial success and working lives. We're not going to find the answers to fix these problems from big corporate frameworks or the pioneers of yesteryear, we need fresh perspectives and energy to make the change.

How can we do this? Experiments and making change. Nigel highlighted a range of places to get started focused on how you support and steer the environment / system. This is the basis of Systems Thinking and Influence Mapping. This focus on understanding how people, teams, incentives, and processes interact. Small iterative tweaks to these are easier, more successful and more likely to develop long running improvements than large frameworks or top-down design. This is the art of Agile Coaching.

It deserves a more focused dive, but what better thing could Nigel use to help us understand the challenges of methods than a joke he made years ago that stuck... the Nigel Scale! Do read into this more and how it helps us dissect Scrum, but in this talk it was used to help us see that many things we consider best practice or essential, really aren't. We need to recognise what is truly core and unbendable, and what can be experimented with and adapted. Surgeons for example disinfect their hands before operations. It doesn't matter on your thoughts and beliefs on bacteria, breaking this will cause things to go horribly wrong. The problem being most things are not like this, and need flexibility, experimentation and adaption to the context. This is a key problem for large methodologies as to much gets locked in as core and unchangeable, and in turn fail because they can’t work within our context.

Nigel goes on to talk about how different methods can mature or sour over time, and change states in on scale. Story points can be amazing for a intermediate team, but go mouldy over time. This is something I want to dive into in a future post.

Wrapping up Nigel is a man after my own heart and recommended forming a rebel alliance, a coup! He pointed to the Maquis, French resistance fighters organising themselves for guerrilla warfare in World War II. All the same reactive patterns with small and distributed scale. Agile as a word may be toxic right now, but it can be rehabilitated. Even without the word the root practices of focused on iterative, collaborative, empowered work all still function, we just need to wrap it up differently.

### Wrap up

The core message for me is that regardless the branding we need to keep learning, experimenting, and challenging orthodoxy to be successful.

I always find talks like this in an in-person community session like this deeply inspirational. Its fills my cup with enthusiasm and makes me want to be part of the change to make things better. I highly recommend making time to talk with people face to face on topics like this be it via Agile Glos or any other means.

I learned a lot about the history of the movement, the depths of our current problems and how wider society is impacting our working climate. Next, I'm going to continuing to build communities wherever I can to find better ideas, approaches, as well as looking into how some are better or worse at different stages in a team's life cycle.
